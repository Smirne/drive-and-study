TOGAF Enterprise Architecture Foundation. Episode one. The exam, and the big picture.

Welcome. This short audio course prepares you for the TOGAF Enterprise Architecture Foundation exam, also called Part 1, based on the TOGAF Standard, tenth edition. There are eight episodes. Listen to them in order the first time, then replay the ones that feel weakest.

Let's start with the exam itself, because it shapes how you should study. The Foundation exam has forty multiple choice questions. You have sixty minutes. It is closed book, so unlike Part 2 you cannot look anything up. The pass mark is sixty percent, which means twenty four correct answers out of forty. The questions test recall and understanding: definitions, the objectives of each phase, the purpose of each technique, and which deliverable belongs where. So the winning strategy is precise vocabulary. Many wrong options are almost right, and differ by a single word. Throughout this course, whenever you hear a definition, try to repeat it in your head.

Now, the big picture. What is an enterprise? In TOGAF, an enterprise is the highest level of description of an organization, and typically covers all missions and functions. An enterprise can span multiple organizations. It could be a whole corporation, a single division, or even a group of companies working together in a supply chain. The key idea is that the enterprise is whatever scope you decide to architect.

What is architecture? TOGAF gives two meanings. First, the fundamental concepts or properties of a system in its environment, embodied in its elements, relationships, and in the principles of its design and evolution. Second, depending on context, the structure of components, their inter-relationships, and the principles and guidelines governing their design and evolution over time. Remember both: the first is the formal, ISO style definition; the second is the more practical one.

And Enterprise Architecture? It describes the structure and behavior of the enterprise, so that it can be changed in a controlled way. Why bother? TOGAF lists benefits such as more efficient business operations, more efficient IT operations, better return on existing investment and reduced risk for future investment, and faster, simpler and cheaper procurement. In one phrase: Enterprise Architecture helps the organization achieve the right balance between business transformation and continuous operational efficiency.

An architecture framework is a conceptual structure used to plan, develop, implement, govern and sustain an architecture. It should contain a method for designing the target state, a common vocabulary, recommended standards, and ideally a set of tools. TOGAF is exactly that: a framework, developed and maintained by The Open Group.

Next: how is the TOGAF Standard, tenth edition, organized? This is a classic exam question. The standard has two parts. The first is the TOGAF Fundamental Content. These are the universal, stable concepts that apply to any organization. The second is the TOGAF Series Guides. These give more specific, practical guidance on particular topics, such as agile, digital, security, business architecture, value streams and so on. The Series Guides can evolve faster than the fundamental content. Together, with other documents, they live in the wider TOGAF Library.

The Fundamental Content is split into six documents. One: Introduction and Core Concepts. Two: Architecture Development Method, the A D M. Three: A D M Techniques. Four: Applying the A D M. Five: Architecture Content. Six: Enterprise Architecture Capability and Governance. A simple memory hook: concepts, method, techniques, applying, content, capability. The tenth edition was redesigned this way to make the standard modular and easier to adopt in parts.

Now, the four architecture domains. TOGAF considers four types of architecture, commonly accepted as subsets of an overall Enterprise Architecture. First, Business Architecture: the business strategy, governance, organization, and key business processes. Second, Data Architecture: the structure of the organization's logical and physical data assets and data management resources. Third, Application Architecture: a blueprint for the individual applications to be deployed, their interactions, and their relationships to the core business processes. Fourth, Technology Architecture: the software and hardware capabilities required to support the deployment of business, data and application services, including IT infrastructure, middleware, networks, communications, processing and standards. Data and Application together are called the Information Systems Architectures. You'll hear that phrase again in Phase C.

Next idea: levels of architecture, also called the Architecture Landscape. Because an enterprise is large, architecture is described at different levels of detail. There are three. The Strategic Architecture is a summary, long term view of the whole enterprise, used for direction setting at the executive level. The Segment Architecture is a more detailed view of an area of the enterprise, such as a business unit or a portfolio, used at program or portfolio level. The Capability Architecture is the most detailed, describing how the enterprise can support a particular unit of capability, and is used for project level work. Strategic, Segment, Capability: from wide and shallow to narrow and deep.

Finally, the idea of an Enterprise Architecture Capability. TOGAF does not just describe how to produce architectures. It also describes how to establish and operate the practice that produces them: the people, the processes, the governance, the repository and the tools. This is what the Preliminary Phase sets up, and it is covered in the sixth document of the Fundamental Content.

Let's recap episode one. The exam: forty questions, sixty minutes, closed book, pass at twenty four. An enterprise is whatever scope you architect, and can span organizations. Architecture has two definitions, the ISO style one and the structural one. The TOGAF Standard has Fundamental Content, in six documents, plus Series Guides. There are four domains: Business, Data, Application, Technology, with Data and Application forming Information Systems. And three landscape levels: Strategic, Segment, Capability.
